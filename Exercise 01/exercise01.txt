2. PART (a): SYNCHRONIZATION
With synchronization: 0.0005760 s; verification 0/512 mismatches.
Without synchronization: 0.0000939 s; verification 0/512 mismatches.
The launch is asynchronous. Synchronization before stopping the clock
ensures GPU completion is included. Without it, the time does not
guarantee completed work. The later device-to-host copy waits for the
result, which explains why verification still passes.

Ex3: 512 blocks x 1 thread. Ex4: 1 block x 512 threads.
Ex5: 10 million elements, 19532 blocks x 512 threads.
Ex6: 100 million elements, 313 x 625 blocks of 32 x 16 threads.
All 12 logs report zero mismatches. Timers include launch overhead,
error checks and synchronization; they exclude copies and verification.
No explicit warm-up was performed; first-launch overhead and timer
resolution can matter, especially for the short 512-element tests.

PREDICTIONS AND OBSERVATIONS
Full warps favor the 512-thread block, although one block uses only one
multiprocessor. It was slightly faster on average in this dataset.
The 100-million-element case took 8.71 times the 10-million case
for ten times as many elements. Memory bandwidth, utilization and
measurement overhead affect scaling.

4. PART (c): KERNEL VERSUS WHOLE SEQUENCE
100 million elements, all verified correctly.
Kernel: 0.004692793 s.
Copy-in, compute, copy-out sequence: 0.437891006 s.
Ratio: 93.31x; difference: 0.433198213 s.
Allocation, random input generation, printing and verification are
outside both intervals. The difference mainly reflects transfers and
their overhead, not an independently measured pure transfer time.

THINK
Transfers dominate this simple operation. More computation per element,
or keeping data on the GPU across multiple operations, can make GPU
execution more worthwhile. Timings alone here do not establish CPU/GPU
speedup because no equivalent CPU workload was timed.
